# Passenger Rights Guide: EU261 & US DOT — A Cancelled-Flight Scenario

**Prepared by Marco, Senior Travel Agent — worked example for a fictional client**

Scenario: a client is booked on a fictional flight, "AeroLinx Flight AL204,"
Barcelona (BCN) to Chicago O'Hare (ORD), scheduled to depart at 11:40, but
the airline cancels it the evening before due to a crew scheduling issue
and rebooks the client on a flight two days later. The client wants to know
what they're owed, and under which framework, given the flight touches
both an EU departure point and a US destination.

## Step One: Work Out Which Framework Applies

This is the question Marco always asks first, because EU261 and US DOT
rules are triggered differently and pay out very differently.

- **EU261 applies** when a flight *departs* from an EU/EEA airport (any
  airline), or when it *arrives* in the EU/EEA on an EU/EEA-based carrier.
  A flight departing Barcelona qualifies under EU261 regardless of where
  it's operated by or where it's going, because the departure point is in
  the EU.
- **US DOT rules apply** primarily to flights within, into, or out of the
  US, but historically offer far weaker cancellation compensation than
  EU261 — the US framework focuses mainly on refund rights, not fixed
  cash compensation for cancellations.

**For this scenario:** because AL204 departs from Barcelona, EU261 applies
and takes precedence for compensation purposes, even though the
destination is a US airport. This is the detail clients most often miss —
they assume "the airline is not European" or "we're flying to the US" means
EU261 doesn't apply. It's the departure airport that matters here.

## What EU261 Actually Owes for a Cancellation

EU261 compensation depends on two things: how far in advance the airline
notified the passenger, and the flight distance.

| Notice given | Compensation owed |
|---|---|
| 14+ days before departure | None required (beyond re-routing/refund options) |
| 7-13 days before, AND re-routing arrives within 4 hours of original time | Compensation may be reduced by 50% |
| Under 14 days' notice, no qualifying re-routing exception | Full compensation applies |

For distance-based compensation amounts (flights over 3,500km, which
BCN–ORD is):

| Flight distance | Standard compensation |
|---|---|
| Under 1,500km | €250 |
| 1,500-3,500km | €400 |
| Over 3,500km (like BCN–ORD) | €600 |

**In this scenario:** the client was notified the evening before departure
— well under the 14-day threshold, and the replacement flight two days
later does not qualify for any reduction exception (it's nowhere near
arriving within 4 hours of the original time). The client is entitled to
**€600 in compensation per passenger**, in addition to:

- **Care and assistance** while waiting — meals, refreshments, and hotel
  accommodation with transport to/from the hotel, since the delay involves
  an overnight wait.
- **Right to choose** between rebooking (which the airline already
  arranged) or a full refund of the unused ticket if the client no longer
  wants to travel.

## Exceptions That Would Change This

EU261 compensation does **not** apply if the airline can prove the
cancellation was due to "extraordinary circumstances" — genuinely
unforeseeable events outside the airline's control, such as severe
weather, air traffic control strikes, or security threats. A crew
scheduling issue, as in this scenario, is generally **not** considered
extraordinary — it's treated as within the airline's operational control,
which is why the client's claim should succeed. If the airline instead
cited a named storm grounding flights across the region, that would likely
qualify as extraordinary and compensation would not be owed (though care
and assistance obligations would still apply).

## What US DOT Rules Would Add (If the Return Leg Cancelled Instead)

If instead the *return* flight, ORD–BCN, had been cancelled by a US-based
carrier, EU261 would not apply (arrival in the EU on a non-EU carrier does
not trigger it), and the client would fall under the weaker US DOT
framework:

- **Guaranteed refund rights**: if the airline cancels or "significantly
  changes" a flight and the passenger doesn't want the rebooking offered,
  the passenger is entitled to a full refund to their original payment
  method — this is a firm US DOT rule, not airline discretion.
- **No fixed cash compensation requirement**: unlike EU261, US rules do not
  mandate a fixed compensation payment for cancellations. Some airlines
  voluntarily offer meal or hotel vouchers for cancellations within their
  control, tracked via the DOT's airline customer service dashboard, but
  this varies airline by airline and is not a legal entitlement in the way
  EU261 compensation is.
- **Tarmac delay rules** are a separate, narrower US protection covering
  extended delays on the tarmac specifically, not general cancellations.

**Marco's takeaway for clients flying transatlantic:** the departure
airport is the single most important fact for figuring out passenger
rights — a flight departing the EU carries strong, fixed compensation
rights regardless of the operating airline, while a flight departing the
US relies on the weaker refund-only DOT framework unless the airline
itself chooses to offer more.

## How Marco Tells Clients to Actually Claim

1. Keep the cancellation notice/email and boarding pass or booking
   reference — the claim needs proof of the booking and the cancellation.
2. Contact the airline directly first, citing EU261 by regulation name and
   the specific compensation tier (in this case, €600 for a long-haul
   cancellation with insufficient notice).
3. If the airline refuses or goes quiet, escalate to the national
   enforcement body for the country where the flight departed (in this
   case, Spain's AESA), or use a no-win-no-fee claims company as a last
   resort, accepting they take a percentage.

## Verification Note

Passenger rights frameworks, compensation amounts, and airline-specific
policies can change, and the "extraordinary circumstances" exception is
frequently contested case by case. This guide reflects the general
structure of EU261 and US DOT rules as commonly applied; always verify the
current regulation text and, for a live claim, check the airline's own
published policy and the relevant national enforcement body before
proceeding.
