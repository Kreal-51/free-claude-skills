# Example Output

**User prompt:** "I run a small design studio as an LLC, cash basis. Last month I bought a new laptop for $1,800 on the business credit card, and I also got a $3,000 client payment deposited straight into my business checking account. Can you give me the journal entries and also help me understand what these do to my books?"

**Response:**

**Basis confirmed:** Cash basis, LLC. Entries below reflect cash-basis recognition - revenue recorded when received, expense recorded when the liability/purchase occurs on the card.

**Entry 1 - Laptop purchase on business credit card**

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment (Fixed Asset) | $1,800 | |
| Business Credit Card (Liability) | | $1,800 |

Plain English: the laptop is a fixed asset, not a straight expense, because it's a piece of equipment expected to last beyond one year. You now owe $1,800 on the card until it's paid off. Note: depending on your tax situation, this may be eligible for Section 179 or bonus depreciation treatment instead of being depreciated over several years - that election is a CPA/tax question, not a bookkeeping one, so flag it for your CPA at tax time rather than assuming.

**Entry 2 - Client payment received**

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Business Checking (Asset) | $3,000 | |
| Service Revenue | | $3,000 |

Plain English: cash basis means this counts as revenue the moment it lands in your account, regardless of when you did the work or sent the invoice.

**What this does to your books this month:**
- Assets go up: $3,000 cash in, and a $1,800 laptop now sits on your balance sheet as equipment.
- Liabilities go up: $1,800 owed on the credit card until you pay it off.
- Revenue goes up: $3,000, which will show on this month's P&L and increase your net income (and your eventual tax liability on that income).
- The laptop purchase does NOT reduce this month's P&L by $1,800 the way a normal expense would, because it's capitalized as an asset, not expensed immediately. If you were expecting the laptop to lower this month's taxable income, it won't, at least not yet.

**Next steps:** When you pay off the credit card, that's a separate entry: debit the card liability, credit business checking. And confirm with your CPA how they want to handle the laptop for tax purposes (full deduction now vs. depreciated over time) - that decision affects your books but the correct treatment is a tax question, not something to guess at here.
